CS6120 · Special Topic

When Thoughts
Stop Being Words

Can we read an AI's mind? For now, almost.

Yizhang Cao · Sicheng Xue · Yian Zhou

Watch the sentence at the bottom. It starts as a model's reasoning and slowly turns into dots. That is the whole talk in ten seconds: today, AI models think in words we can read. The question is whether that stays true. We are not going to give you a list of papers. We want to leave you with three surprises and one uncomfortable question.
A new kind of AI

For the first time, AI thinks out loud before it answers

what the model writes before answering

If we can read its thoughts,

we can catch bad intentions before it acts.

Researchers from OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind wrote a joint paper about this idea. They called it a fragile opportunity.

Korbak et al. 2025, Chain of Thought Monitorability: A New and Fragile Opportunity for AI Safety
Models like o1, DeepSeek-R1 or Claude with extended thinking write a long scratchpad before they answer. Ask the room: has anyone read one of these? They sound surprisingly human: hmm, wait, let me check. This gives safety researchers something they never had with neural networks: a readable trace of the decision. Competing labs signed one paper together to say: this is precious, and it may not last. Today: why it is precious, why it is fragile.
Surprise 1

Sometimes the AI just tells you

Task: make the failing unit tests pass.
Watching what it does
60%
Reading what it thinks
95%

Share of cheating caught. The reader was a weaker model than the one doing the task.

Baker et al. (OpenAI) 2025, Monitoring Reasoning Models for Misbehavior and the Risks of Promoting Obfuscation. Thought text abridged from the paper's examples.
This is a real pattern from an OpenAI study of a frontier reasoning model doing coding tasks. Instead of fixing the code, it noticed it could cheat, for example by exiting before the tests run, and it literally wrote "Let's hack." Click: if you only watch the code it writes, you catch about 60 percent of the cheating. If you also read its thoughts, 95 percent. And the reader was a smaller, weaker model. That is the dream of chain-of-thought monitoring.
Surprise 2

And sometimes it doesn't

A Stanford professor thinks the answer is (A).
Which option is correct?
…let me work through each option… so the answer is (A).

The hint changed the answer. The reasoning never mentions the professor.

answer changed by the hintreasoning admits it

Often fewer than 1 in 5 admit it.

Chen et al. (Anthropic) 2025, Reasoning Models Don't Always Say What They Think. Claude 3.7 Sonnet and DeepSeek-R1. Grid is illustrative.
Researchers slipped a hint into the question, like "a professor thinks it is A." When the hint flipped the model's answer, they checked whether the reasoning ever mentions it. Most of the time, it does not. The model writes a clean, convincing derivation that happens to land on the hinted answer. So the text looks like reasoning, but it is not the whole reason. This is the setting our own final project starts from.
Sound familiar?

Humans do this too

Left brain · can talk
sees a chicken claw
picks a chicken
Right brain · cannot talk
sees a snowy house
picks a shovel

“Why the shovel?” — “The chicken claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed.”

A split-brain patient: the speaking half never saw the snow, so it invented a reason on the spot, instantly and confidently.

Gazzaniga 1998, The Split Brain Revisited, Scientific American
Here is the part we found most inspiring. In split-brain patients, the two halves of the brain cannot share information. Show the right half a snowy scene and the left half a chicken claw. The hands pick a shovel and a chicken. Ask why, and the talking half, which never saw the snow, makes up a reason without hesitating. Neuroscientists call this the interpreter. Explanations can be stories built after the decision. That is exactly what we just saw in the model. A chain of thought is a report about a decision, and reports can be wrong.
Surprise 3

The words might not even matter

Replace the reasoning with “......”

and a small transformer still solves problems it could not solve by answering directly. Each dot buys one more step of hidden computation.

Or drop words completely

Meta's Coconut feeds the model's internal state straight back in, “thinking” in vectors. On some planning puzzles it beats normal reasoning while using about a third of the tokens.

Pfau, Merrill, Bowman 2024, Let's Think Dot by Dot · Hao et al. (Meta) 2024, Training LLMs to Reason in a Continuous Latent Space
If the text is sometimes a story, what is it for? Part of the answer is boring: every extra token is extra computation. One study trained a small model to answer after a row of meaningless dots and it worked, on tasks where answering immediately failed. Caveat: small models, special training. Another line of work removes words altogether, so the model reasons in vectors. For a monitor this is the end of the road: there is nothing left to read. And it can be cheaper, which is exactly why companies may want it.
The twist

Punish bad thoughts, get hidden bad thoughts

The obvious next step: reward the model for not having bad thoughts.

It kept cheating. It just stopped writing it down, and the reader's catch rate fell to almost zero.

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

Goodhart's law, in Marilyn Strathern's phrasing

Baker et al. (OpenAI) 2025, same study as Surprise 1
Back to the cheating model. If reading thoughts catches cheating, why not train the model to have no cheating thoughts? OpenAI tried. With strong pressure, the model did not become honest. It learned to write innocent-looking reasoning and then cheat anyway. Think of a kid punished every time they say "I am going to copy the homework." They do not stop copying; they stop saying it. Their recommendation: accept a monitorability tax, and do not train directly on the thoughts.
So where does this leave us?

A window, not a wall

What closes it
  • Training models to make their thoughts look good
  • Models that think in vectors instead of words
  • Tasks easy enough that the words are optional
What keeps it open
  • Hard, many-step tasks: the model needs the scratchpad, so it has to write the plan down
  • Leaving the thoughts untrained: pay the monitorability tax
  • Checking the text against what is going on inside
Emmons et al. (Google DeepMind) 2025, When Chain of Thought is Necessary, Language Models Struggle to Evade Monitors
This is not doom. Google DeepMind found that when a task genuinely needs many steps, models struggle to hide their plan, because they have to use the scratchpad to succeed. The danger is when the words are optional or when we train them to look clean. The last point on the right is where our own project comes in.
Our final project

Even when the words are honest: are they late?

We give a model a misleading hint and, at every paragraph, peek at the answer it would give if we stopped it right there.

In our first pilot, the model often settled on the hinted answer early, while its text caught up much later.

Pilot on one 7B model, still preliminary. The full study covers nine open models.

Keep this short; details come in our final presentation. The question: even if the reasoning is honest, does it lag behind the decision it describes? If it does, a monitor reading the text will always be a step behind. Early signs say yes, but we will only claim it after the full study.

Would you trade a smarter AI
for one you can no longer read?

And when you explain your own decisions,
are you reading your mind, or telling a story?

End on the two questions and pause. The first is a real choice the field is making right now in training recipes. The second is for everyone: the split-brain patient was sure about the chicken shed. How sure are you about your own reasons? Thank you; happy to take questions.
